## The Beginning: An Announcement That Changed Computing

On September 27, 1983, Richard Stallman posted a message to mailing lists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), announcing his intention to build a complete Unix-compatible operating system called GNU (a recursive acronym for "GNU's Not Unix"). The goal was clear: to create a completely free operating system that grants users the freedom to use, study, modify, and distribute software, without the restrictions of proprietary software.

At the time, most software was closed source, and users were forbidden from modifying or sharing it. Stallman, who worked at MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, saw how proprietary software had divided the developer community that once shared code freely. His decision to launch GNU was not just a technical project. It was an ethical and political statement.

## The Record: What Has GNU Achieved?

Forty-three years later, it is fair to say that GNU has achieved success beyond all expectations. Here are its most notable accomplishments:

### System Tools That Changed Computing

When you open a terminal on any Linux distribution today, you are using GNU tools with every command you type. Tools like `ls`, `cat`, `grep`, `awk`, `sed`, `make`, `bash`, `gcc`, and `gdb` are part of GNU's legacy. These are not just ordinary programs. They are the backbone of any free development environment.

### The GNU Project in Numbers

- **Core GNU tools:** Coreutils, Bash, GCC, GDB, Make, Autoconf, Gnulib, and hundreds of other tools.
- **GNU GPL:** The GNU General Public License, the most famous free software license in the world, written in a way that ensures software remains free forever.
- **Associated projects:** GNOME, GIMP, Emacs, GNU Guix, GNU Boot (which recently joined the FSF's fiscal sponsorship program), and many others.
- **Impact on Linux:** The Linux kernel developed by Linus Torvalds, which combined with GNU tools to form the GNU/Linux system, running on millions of servers and devices worldwide.

### The GPL: A Legal Revolution

No discussion of GNU's achievements is complete without mentioning the GPL. This license, crafted with legal brilliance, uses copyright law to guarantee the four freedoms: running the program for any purpose, studying and modifying it, redistributing it, and distributing modified versions. Today, the GPL is used in thousands of projects worldwide, from kernels to applications.

## The Failures: Where Did the Dream Stumble?

Despite its spectacular successes, not everything Stallman dreamed of came to pass. There are failures and challenges that must be acknowledged:

### The GNU Kernel (Hurd): The Unfinished Project

Hurd was supposed to be the kernel of the GNU system. But its development stalled for decades due to the complexity of its microkernel design and other technical challenges. Meanwhile, the Linux kernel appeared in 1991 and filled the gap. This led to the current situation where the complete operating system is known as GNU/Linux, not GNU/Hurd.

### GNU's Failure to Break Into the Desktop

Although GNU tools run on millions of servers, the GNU/Linux system has not achieved the widespread adoption on desktop computers that was hoped for. Windows and macOS still dominate the personal computer market, while Linux remains a system for specialists and enthusiasts.

### The Software Ecosystem: Internal Divisions

The free software community has faced internal divisions over the years. Some were technical, such as the split between GNOME and KDE. Others were ideological, such as the divide between "Free Software" and "Open Source." These divisions weakened the movement's unified voice.

## Current Challenges: The Age of Cloud and AI

In 2026, the free software movement faces new challenges that did not exist in 1983:

### Cloud Computing: When Software Becomes a Service

Many free software programs today are used as cloud services (SaaS), meaning the user does not receive a copy of the program but uses it over the internet. This threatens the principle of "freedom" that Stallman fought for, because the user cannot control the software they are using. The AGPL (GNU Affero General Public License) was created to partially address this problem, but it is not a complete solution.

### Artificial Intelligence: Will the Model Be Open?

With the rise of large AI models, a new question emerges: are "open source" models truly open? Most models described as "open" are actually "open weight," where the weights are available for download, but the training data and training code remain secret. This new challenge reminds us that GNU's principles are still alive and necessary, and need to be applied to new technologies.

### Legal and Political Challenges

The free software movement faces pressure from patent laws, international trade agreements, and attempts to restrict the use of free software for security purposes. These challenges test the resilience of the licenses and alliances the movement has built over decades.

## Summary: A Legacy Still Alive

Forty-three years later, GNU is still a living, breathing project. Its tools run on millions of servers, its GPL license protects user freedoms, and its projects are expanding into new fields. Yes, Hurd was never completed, the desktop was not opened up as hoped, and new challenges keep appearing. But the original essence of the GNU project, that software must be free to serve humans and not the other way around, remains valid and more urgent than ever.

On GNU's birthday, we celebrate not only the technical achievements, but the ethical idea it launched: that software freedom is an inseparable part of human freedom in the digital age.
